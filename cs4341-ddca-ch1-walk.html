That is the page's first half: one architecture, many microarchitectures with different trade-offs.

    Key points

    • Architecture is the programmer's view of a computer: the instructions and registers a program may use. x86 is one architecture.
    • Microarchitecture is how logic carries out those instructions; one architecture can have many microarchitectures differing in price, performance and power.
    • Discipline means deliberately limiting design choices so you can work faster at a higher level, the way standard interchangeable parts sped up rifle making.
    • Digital circuits use discrete voltages and analog circuits continuous ones; digital is the narrower class but much easier to design and combine.

    Check. Two chips run the same compiled program unchanged; one is fast and costly, the other slow and cheap. Do they share an architecture, a microarchitecture, or both?

    Answer. They share an architecture (the same instructions and registers) but have different microarchitectures.

    The same program runs on both, so the programmer's view matches; price and speed differ because the logic that carries out the instructions differs.

    Needs a video that teaches hierarchy, modularity and regularity with a drawn or building example: split a system into modules and submodules until each is easy to understand; give every module one clear job and a clean boundary, so modules connect without surprises; reuse identical modules.     Key points

    • Digital systems often beat analog ones because simple parts combine easily: digital TV, CDs and cell phones replaced their analog versions.
    • Hierarchy: split a system into modules, then split those again, until every piece is easy to understand.

1.3 The Digital Abstraction

  1. pdf32p.7

    The digital abstraction: continuous vs discrete values

    1:08–2:50YouTube app

    Introduction to Digital Electronics
    ALL ABOUT ELECTRONICS, plays 1:42 of 10:43

    ALL ABOUT ELECTRONICS defines an analog signal as one whose amplitude can take any value in a range (infinitely many possibilities) and a digital signal as one whose amplitude takes only a finite number of values. That is the page's continuous vs discrete-valued split.

    Key points

    • Most physical quantities are continuous: a wire's voltage, an oscillation's frequency or a mass's position can take any value in a range.
    • A digital system stores information in discrete-valued variables: each one can take only a finite set of separate values.
    • Babbage's Analytical Engine was an early digital machine: each gear had ten positions, 0 to 9, much like a car's odometer.

    Check. A dimmer knob can sit at any brightness between off and full; a fan switch clicks into off, low, medium or high. Which one is continuous, which is discrete-valued, and how many distinct values does the fan switch have?

    Answer. The dimmer is continuous; the fan switch is discrete-valued, with 4 distinct values.

    A discrete-valued variable has a finite list of distinct values; the dimmer has infinitely many in its range.

    • Trap. The video also says continuous and discrete about TIME: a temperature read once an hour is a discrete-time signal but still analog (frame 1:53). The book's discrete is about the VALUES: a finite set of distinct values is what makes a variable digital.
    • Trap. The video's examples are voice, temperature, pressure and an amplifier output between 0 and 10V; the book's continuous examples are a wire's voltage, an oscillation's frequency and a mass's position.
    • Trap. The video's digital example (frame 2:41) jumps between two levels, but digital does not have to mean two: the book's opening example of a digital machine, Babbage's engine, used gears with ten values.

    Read in the book, not in the video: Babbage's Analytical Engine (designed 1834 to 1871; gears with ten positions, 0 to 9, like a car odometer), the Babbage sidebar, and the Figure 1.2 rifle picture.
