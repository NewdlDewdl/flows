    0:00–5:20YouTube app

    n items go to r persons, ni to person i (0:00-1:19). Order all n items, n! ways; each split comes from n1! ··· nr! of those orders (1:19-3:52). So the count is n!/(n1! ··· nr!), the multinomial coefficient (3:55-4:18); with r = 2 it is the binomial coefficient (4:21 on).

    Key points

    • Example 1.31: choose the leader (n ways), then any subset of the other n - 1 people (2n-1 ways): n 2n-1 clubs. By club size instead: the sum over k of k (n choose k).
    • A partition splits an n-element set into r labeled groups of sizes n1, ..., nr, which add up to n. A combination is the r = 2 case.
    • Form the groups one at a time: (n choose n1)(n - n1 choose n2) ···. The factorials cancel to n!/(n1! n2! ··· nr!), the multinomial coefficient.

    Check. In how many ways can 9 people be split into a group of 4, a group of 3 and a group of 2 (the groups are labeled)?

    Answer. 9!/(4! 3! 2!) = 1260.

    Choose the 4, then 3 of the remaining 5, then the last 2: (9 choose 4)(5 choose 3)(2 choose 2) = 126 · 10 · 1 = 1260, the multinomial coefficient.

    Independent random variables; conditional independence; E[XY]

    2:13–5:08YouTube app

    L07.4 Independence of Random Variables
    MIT OpenCourseWare, plays 2:55 of 5:08

    {X = x} and {Y = y} are independent for every x and y, so the joint PMF factors (2:36-2:55), and conditioning on Y leaves the PMF of X unchanged, and the other way round (2:57-3:52). For three or more, the joint PMF is the product of all the marginals (3:56-4:21). Picture separate experiments that do not interact (4:24-5:02).

    Key points

    • X and Y are independent if pX,Y(x, y) = pX(x) pY(y) for all x and y, the same as pX|Y(x | y) = pX(x) wherever pY(y) > 0.
    • Condition on an event A (P(A) > 0): X and Y are conditionally independent if P(X = x, Y = y | A) = P(X = x | A) P(Y = y | A) for all x, y.
    • Neither kind of independence implies the other (Figure 2.15, next page).
    • Independent X and Y: E[XY] = E[X] E[Y]. Factor the joint PMF inside the double sum and it splits into the two means.

    Check. X and Y are independent, pX(1) = 0.3 and pY(2) = 0.5. What are pX,Y(1, 2) and pX|Y(1 | 2)?

    Answer. pX,Y(1, 2) = 0.15; pX|Y(1 | 2) = 0.3.

    Independence: the joint PMF is the product, 0.3 · 0.5, and conditioning on Y leaves the PMF of X as it was.

    Read in the book, not in the video: Conditional independence given an event A (the next card plays Figure 2.15) and the calculation E[XY] = E[X] E[Y] at the bottom of the page (not on this walk).

    L07.7 Independence, Variances & the Binomial Variance
    MIT OpenCourseWare, plays 4:59 of 7:09

    In general the variance of a sum is not the sum of the variances (0:20-0:29); for independent X and Y it is (0:32-0:41). The proof expands E[(X + Y)2], and the middle term 2E[XY] = 2E[X]E[Y] is 0 (0:47-2:45).

    Key points

    • The cross term E[(X - E[X])(Y - E[Y])] is 0: the shifted variables are independent and have mean 0.
    • Means of a sum always add, independent or not; variances add when the variables are independent.
    • Summary: X independent of A; X and Y independent (pX,Y = pX pY); then E[XY] = E[X] E[Y], E[g(X) h(Y)] = E[g(X)] E[h(Y)], and var (X + Y) = var (X) + var (Y).
    • More than two: the definitions extend; three variables X, Y, Z are independent when pX,Y,Z is the product of the three marginals (next page).

    Check. X and Y are independent, var (X) = 2 and var (Y) = 3. What is var (X - 2Y)?

    Answer. 2 + 4 · 3 = 14.

    X and -2Y are independent, so the variances add, and var (-2Y) = (-2)2 var (Y) = 4 var (Y).

    • Trap. The video assumes both means are 0, 'just for the sake of the derivation' (0:47-0:52). The book drops that assumption by working with X - E[X] and Y - E[Y], which have the same variances. Same algebra.

    Read in the book, not in the video: The paragraph on independence beyond two variables (it continues on page 113). The video's last two minutes, the binomial variance np(1 - p), are Example 2.20 on page 113.
