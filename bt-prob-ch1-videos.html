  2. pdf 27 · p.17
    A short history: Pascal and Fermat to Kolmogorov
    Section 1.2 sidebar

    Making Probability Mathematical | Infinite Series · PBS Infinite Series · 3:31 of 14:21

    1:02–2:43 before 1600, then Pascal and FermatYouTube app
    3:47–5:37 scattered results, then Kolmogorov's axiomsYouTube app

    Part 1: dice and gambling for centuries with no math, then the Pascal and Fermat letters about splitting the pot of an unfinished game. Part 2: results pile up with no common foundation until Kolmogorov's 1933 axioms; the square analogy shows why a theory runs on its axioms alone, which is the page's last bullet.

    • The video says nobody treated probability mathematically before 1600; the page credits Cardano (16th century) with the first book of correct methods for dice and cards. Go with the page.
    • Only on the page, not in the video: Jacob Bernoulli's law of large numbers, de Moivre's normal distribution and first central limit theorem, Laplace, Legendre and Gauss (least squares), Poisson, Chebyshev with Markov and Lyapunov.
    • The page's 19th-century point: probabilities were read as limits of relative frequencies, and Kolmogorov's axioms replaced that foundation. The video only says the earlier ideas were scattered.
    • Skipped on purpose: Pascal's wager (2:43-3:47) and the measure-theory dartboard (5:37 on). Neither is on the page.
  3. pdf 28 · p.18
    Conditional probability: why, and the definition
    Section 1.3

    L02.2 Conditional Probabilities · MIT OpenCourseWare · 8:50 of 8:59

    0:00–8:50YouTube app

    The book's author builds the revised model you need once you learn B happened: equally likely outcomes first, then unequal ones, then the formula, which he stresses is a definition, not a theorem. Same order as the page.

    • The video's first picture has 12 equally likely outcomes; the page's example is a fair die: P(6 | even) = 1/3, one outcome out of 2, 4, 6.
    • P(B) > 0 is required (said at 7:38); the page says so at the top of the next page.
    • Read P(A | B) as A given B: the event you know happened goes right of the bar.
  4. pdf 29 · p.19
    Conditional probabilities obey all three axioms
    Section 1.3

    L02.4 Conditional Probabilities Obey the Same Axioms · MIT OpenCourseWare · 7:44 of 7:44

    0:00–7:44YouTube app

    Checks nonnegativity, P(Ω | B) = 1, P(B | B) = 1 (B as the new universe) and additivity for two disjoint events, the same derivation as the page, then says every formula for probabilities carries over to conditional ones.

    • The page adds one worked consequence the video skips: P(A ∪ C | B) ≤ P(A | B) + P(C | B).
    • Countable additivity is argued the same way and not proved, in both.
  5. pdf 30 · p.20
    Summary box and Example 1.6: count inside B
    Section 1.3

    Intro to Conditional Probability | Probability Theory · Wrath of Math · 7:57 of 11:02

    0:17–8:14YouTube app

    Two coin flips: given the first is heads, keep only HH and HT, so at least one tail is 1 of 2 equally likely outcomes; the formula agrees, (1/4)/(1/2) = 1/2. A card example (king given red, 1/13) repeats the counting. That is the box's last two bullets and the shortcut in Example 1.6.

    • Two flips in the video, three tosses in the book: Example 1.6 asks for more heads than tails given the first toss is a head, answer 3/4. Same method: keep only the outcomes in B, count those also in A.
    • Letters differ: the video writes P(E | F), the book P(A | B).
    • The 3/4 written near the top of the video's page is the chance of at least one tail with no condition, not the answer.
    • Counting works only when the outcomes are equally likely; the box's last bullet says so.
    • Stop at 8:14: right after, the video defines independence, which is Section 1.5 (pdf 44).
  6. pdf 31 · p.21
    Example 1.7: a 4-sided die, max given min = 2
    Section 1.3

    L02.3 A Die Roll Example · MIT OpenCourseWare · 5:01 of 5:02

    0:00–5:01YouTube app

    The page's Example 1.7 itself: 16 equally likely outcomes, and B (the smaller roll is 2) holds 5 of them. Max = 1 is impossible, so 0; max = 3 by the formula, (2/16)/(5/16), and by counting, 2 of 5.

    • The video works only max = 1 (0) and max = 3 (2/5). The page also gives max = 2 (1/5) and max = 4 (2/5): count them on Figure 1.8.
    • The video calls the max M and the rolls first and second; the book names the rolls X and Y.
    • The top of the page finishes Example 1.6 (3/4 by counting); the bottom starts Example 1.8, which continues on pdf 32.
