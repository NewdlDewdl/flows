    Two readings of probability: frequency or belief

    0:01–2:45YouTube app

    L01.10 Interpretations & Uses of Probabilities
    MIT OpenCourseWare, plays 2:44 of 3:48

    John Tsitsiklis asks what the word probability means: first a share of heads over endless fair-coin tosses, then a belief about a one-off event (a reelection at 0.7), a guide to bets, and last why subjective beliefs still deserve systematic rules. It is this page's argument with different examples.

    Key points

    • Frequency reading: a probability is the share of times an event happens over many similar repeats, as a fair coin shows heads about half the time.
    • Belief reading: a one-time event (the book's scholar, 90% sure one poet wrote both the Iliad and the Odyssey) cannot repeat, so P states a belief.
    • A frequency needs a track record: an experimental drug this hospital (or this nurse) has never given before has none, so the nurse balks at a number.
    • Beliefs are subjective, yet using them systematically is what lets choices under uncertainty be consistent and, ideally, successful.

    Check. A die is rolled 600 times and shows a six 97 times. (1) What does the frequency reading give as an estimate of P(six), and how close is it to 1/6? (2) A factory finds 1 bolt in 3 comes out bent, and a lawyer is 1/3 sure a signature is forged. Which reading of probability fits each number?

    Answer. (1) 97/600 = 0.162 (rounded), close to 1/6 = 0.167 (rounded). (2) The factory's 1/3 is a frequency over many similar bolts; the forgery is a one-time question with nothing to repeat, so the lawyer's 1/3 is a belief.

    The frequency reading estimates a probability by the share of trials in which the event happened, which needs many similar trials; a question that cannot be repeated, like who signed one document, leaves only the belief reading.

    Key points

    • Set-builder form {x | x satisfies P} keeps the elements of a bigger set that have property P; the bar is read such that (the video writes a colon).
    • S ⊂ T says every element of S also lies in T, and here S = T is allowed; S ⊂ T together with T ⊂ S forces S = T.
    • Inside Ω: Sc is what lies outside S, S ∪ T in S or T or both, S ∩ T in both. For S1, S2, ...: the union holds x in some Sn, the intersection x in every Sn.

    Venn diagrams, set algebra and De Morgan's laws

    0:01–4:52YouTube app

    S01.2 De Morgan's Laws
    MIT OpenCourseWare, plays 4:51 of 4:52

    John Tsitsiklis states the law for the complement of an intersection of two sets, proves it with a Venn picture and then element by element, gets the union version by swapping every set for its complement, and ends with both laws for any collection of sets Sn. That is the De Morgan part of this page, reached by a different route.

    Key points

    • Venn diagrams draw the universal set Ω and the sets inside it as regions, so unions, intersections and complements show up as shaded areas.
    • Set algebra (the book's examples): unions reorder and regroup, ∪ and ∩ distribute over each other; (Sc)c = S, S ∩ Sc = Ø, S ∪ Ω = Ω, S ∩ Ω = S.
    • De Morgan: the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements, and the complement of an intersection is the union of the complements.
    • Both hold for any collection S1, S2, ...: x misses the union exactly when it misses every Sn, i.e. lies in every Snc. Chasing one element proves them.

    Check. Let Ω = {1, 2, ..., 12}, S = the multiples of 2 and T = the multiples of 3 in Ω. Find (S ∪ T)c and Sc ∩ Tc, then (S ∩ T)c and Sc ∪ Tc. Which pairs match, and which law does each pair show?

    Answer. (S ∪ T)c = Sc ∩ Tc = {1, 5, 7, 11}. S ∩ T = {6, 12}, so (S ∩ T)c = Sc ∪ Tc = every number from 1 to 12 except 6 and 12. The first pair is the complement-of-a-union law, the second the complement-of-an-intersection law.

    Outside S ∪ T means outside S and outside T at once; outside S ∩ T means missing at least one of them, so in Sc or in Tc. Taking complements turns each operation into the other.

    Ingredients of a model: sample space and probability law

    Section 1.1, 1.2

    0:03–1:21YouTube app

    L01.2 Sample Space
    MIT OpenCourseWare, plays 1:18 of 5:38

    Tsitsiklis splits building a model into two steps: first describe the possible outcomes, then give a probability law for how likely they are. The stretch then gathers the outcomes into one set, named capital omega and called the sample space: the page's boxed pair of ingredients and the start of Sample Spaces and Events.

    Key points

    • Top lines: De Morgan's union law is finished by running the same argument backward for the other inclusion; the intersection law is proved alike.
    • A probabilistic model has two parts: a sample space Ω, every possible outcome of the experiment, and a probability law defined on events.
    • An event is a subset of Ω, a set of outcomes. The law gives each event A a probability P(A), never negative, for how likely A is to happen.
    • The experiment is the process being modeled: each run produces exactly one outcome, and Ω collects every outcome a run could produce.

    Check. You draw one marble from a bag holding a red, a blue, a green, a white and a black marble, so Ω has those 5 outcomes. Which of these are events: {green}, {red, black}, {blue, yellow}, Ω?

    Answer. {green}, {red, black} and Ω (all five colors) are events. {blue, yellow} is not: no draw can produce yellow, so it is not a subset of Ω.

    An event is a set of outcomes, so it must sit inside Ω. Any set of colors from the bag qualifies, whether it holds one, two or all five; a set holding anything outside Ω does not.

    The three probability axioms, and P(empty set) = 0

    1:27–5:13 events; nonnegativity and normalizationYouTube app

    L01.4 Probability Axioms
    MIT OpenCourseWare, plays 6:24 of 8:55

    Tsitsiklis explains why probabilities go to sets of outcomes (events) rather than single points, then states the axioms: nonnegativity, P(Ω) = 1, and additivity for two disjoint sets, pictured as one pound of substance spread over Ω. The walk skips 5:13-6:17, a reminder of the ∩ and ∪ notation, and the video closes on the remark that P ≤ 1 and other facts follow from the axioms.
